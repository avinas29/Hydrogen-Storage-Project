Part 1Hydrogen storage and metal hydrides

Report: Chapter 1 and §2.1, §2.3–2.4

The physics you need before anything else: why hydrogen is hard to store, what actually happens inside a metal hydride, and why the pressure stays flat (the "plateau") while the metal fills up. Examiners from an energy background usually start here.

Understand

1.1Why storing hydrogen is hard background

Hydrogen carries a lot of energy per kilogram: its lower heating value (LHV) is about 120 MJ/kg (the project uses 119.96 MJ/kg), roughly three times that of petrol. But as a gas at room conditions it is extremely light, so it carries very little energy per litre. Every storage method is a way of packing more hydrogen into a given volume:

MethodHowMain cost
Compressed gassqueeze to several hundred bar in a strong tankcompression energy, heavy/expensive vessel, high-pressure safety
Liquid hydrogencool to about 20 K (−253 °C)liquefaction energy, boil-off, cryogenic insulation
Solid-state (metal hydride)let a metal alloy absorb hydrogen into its crystalweight of the metal, heat management

Metal hydrides store hydrogen at a few bar, close to room temperature, and can pack more hydrogen per litre than liquid hydrogen. Their weakness is mass: the metal is heavy, so only about 1–2% of the weight is hydrogen for the common alloys.

1.2What happens inside a metal hydride background

Picture it
Imagine a crystal of metal atoms as a stack of marbles. Between the marbles there are small gaps ("interstitial sites"). A hydrogen molecule H₂ lands on the surface, splits into two H atoms, and the tiny H atoms slip into the gaps. Fill enough gaps and the metal becomes a new compound, the hydride. Heat it and the H atoms come back out, recombine into H₂ and leave as gas.

The steps, in order: (1) H₂ sticks to the surface (physisorption); (2) it dissociates into 2 H atoms; (3) the atoms diffuse into interstitial sites. Two directions:

  • Absorption (charging): metal + H₂ → hydride. It releases heat (exothermic), so the tank must be cooled while refuelling.
  • Desorption (discharging): hydride → metal + H₂. It needs heat (endothermic), so the tank must be heated to deliver hydrogen. If the heat stops, the hydrogen flow stops, which is a built-in safety feature.

1.3The α phase, the β phase and the plateau background

Read the left plot from left to right, at one fixed temperature, as you push hydrogen in:

  1. α region (left): a little hydrogen dissolves in the metal as a solid solution. Pressure rises quickly with each bit added.
  2. α + β plateau (middle): the hydride phase β starts to form. Now two solid phases coexist, and the pressure stays almost constant while the metal converts from α to β. This flat part is where nearly all the useful storage happens.
  3. β region (right): all metal is hydride; adding more needs a steep pressure rise.
Why is the plateau flat? (Gibbs phase rule)
Phase rule: degrees of freedom \(F = C - P + 2\). On the plateau there are \(C=2\) components (metal, hydrogen) and \(P=3\) phases (α solid, β solid, H₂ gas). So \(F = 2 - 3 + 2 = 1\). Choosing the temperature uses up that one freedom, so the pressure is fixed: it cannot change while α and β coexist. That fixed pressure is the plateau pressure \(P_{eq}\). At a higher temperature the plateau sits higher (the three curves in the figure).

This curve is called the PCI (pressure–composition isotherm) or PCT (pressure–composition–temperature) curve. Measuring it in a Sieverts apparatus (a calibrated gas volume connected to the sample) is the standard experiment.

The one trade-off that explains everything
Light alloys (Mg-based) store more hydrogen per kg but hold it too tightly (high ΔH), so they need high temperatures. Alloys that release hydrogen near room temperature (AB₅, AB₂, AB) are transition-metal alloys: heavy, so only ≈ 1–2 wt%. No material in the data has both high capacity and near-ambient operation.

1.6Engineering properties and their definitions Report §2.3

  • Gravimetric capacity, wt% = mass of H ÷ mass of hydride × 100. Also given as H/M: hydrogen atoms per metal atom, or per formula unit (the Purdue files use per formula unit). Literature values differ in definition: total uptake, reversible capacity between two pressures, or first plateau only.
  • Material vs system capacity: a real tank adds the vessel, a heat exchanger and empty space between powder grains, so the system capacity is much lower than the material's. The DOE 2025 target of 5.5 wt% is a system target.
  • Volumetric capacity (kg H₂ per m³) needs the density of the hydride or the powder bed. It is reported for only 37 Purdue materials, so the project does not estimate it elsewhere.
  • Reaction heat Q (MJ per kg H₂): must be supplied on discharge and removed on refuelling.
  • Kinetics: how fast it absorbs/desorbs. Depends on particle size, surface, catalysts, and how far the pressure is from equilibrium.
  • Cycle life: capacity loss over many cycles. Not available in the datasets.
Defend
Q1.1Why metal hydrides and not compressed gas?

Hydrides store at a few bar near room temperature, so the vessel is cheaper and safer, and the volumetric density is high. The price is weight (≈ 1–2 wt% for common alloys) and the need to manage heat. So they suit stationary storage, forklifts or systems with waste heat, where weight matters less.

Q1.2What is the plateau and why is it flat?

It is the two-phase region where α (solid solution) and β (hydride) coexist. With 2 components and 3 phases, the phase rule gives one degree of freedom, so at a fixed temperature the pressure is fixed. That is the plateau pressure, \(P_{eq}\).

Q1.3What is hysteresis and why does it matter?

Absorption needs a higher pressure than desorption at the same temperature. Each cycle therefore needs extra compression (or a larger temperature swing), which is an energy loss. For TiFe the charging pressure is about 1.9 times the desorption pressure.

Q1.4Is 2 wt% good enough for a car?

Not for a light-duty car. The DOE 2025 target is 5.5 wt% for the whole system, and ≈ 2 wt% is the material alone; a system would be lower still. That is why the report recommends hydrides for uses where mass matters less: forklifts, buses, stationary buffers.

Trap Never present 2 wt% as a tank figure.
Q1.5Why not just use MgH₂? It holds 7.6 wt%.

Its ΔH is ≈ 78 kJ/mol, so it needs ≈ 300 °C to give 1 bar, and the heat needed is ≈ 39 MJ per kg H₂, almost a third of the hydrogen's own energy content. It is also kinetically slow. Fuel-cell waste heat (≈ 70 °C) cannot drive it, so Mg-based materials fail both screens.

Q1.6Why is absorption exothermic?

Hydrogen atoms bound in the lattice are at lower energy than free H₂ gas, so forming the hydride releases energy. Getting them out costs the same energy, which is why desorption needs heat. The amount per mole of H₂ is ΔH.
