Part 2Thermodynamics: the Van't Hoff equation

Report: §2.2 and Chapter 5

One equation connects the two numbers ΔH and ΔS to everything an engineer wants to know: the pressure at a given temperature, the temperature needed for a given pressure, and the heat to supply. Know how to derive it, how to use it with real numbers, and what its limits are.

Understand

2.1What ΔH and ΔS mean physically

ΔH: enthalpy of decomposition
The energy needed to pull one mole of H₂ out of the hydride (kJ per mol H₂). A bigger ΔH means hydrogen is held more tightly, so a higher temperature is needed to release it. LaNi₅: 32 kJ/mol; MgH₂: 78 kJ/mol.
ΔS: entropy of decomposition
Mostly the entropy hydrogen gains when it goes from "locked in the crystal" to "free gas". The standard entropy of H₂ gas is ≈ 130.7 J/(mol·K), so ΔS is similar for most hydrides. In your data the median is 110.6 J/(mol·K), with half of all materials between 102.8 and 118.9.
Sign convention trap
Chemists often quote formation values (metal + H₂ → hydride), which are negative (−32 kJ/mol). This project uses positive desorption values (+32 kJ/mol). Same physics, opposite sign. The Purdue files store negative values in J/mol; HydPARK stores positive values in kJ/mol. Mixing them unconverted would be a disaster, so the audit converted everything to positive kJ/mol (ΔH) and J/(mol·K) (ΔS).

2.2Deriving the Van't Hoff relation

For desorption, \(\tfrac{2}{x}\mathrm{MH}_x \rightleftharpoons \tfrac{2}{x}\mathrm{M} + \mathrm{H_2}\). The Gibbs energy change, with hydrogen at pressure \(P\), is

\[ \Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S + RT\ln\frac{P}{P_0}. \]

At equilibrium on the plateau \(\Delta G = 0\), and \(P = P_{eq}\):

\[ \boxed{\;\ln\frac{P_{eq}}{P_0} = -\frac{\Delta H}{RT} + \frac{\Delta S}{R}\;} \]

\(R = 8.314462618\) J/(mol·K), and \(P_0 = 1\) atm = 1.01325 bar is the reference pressure (both datasets use 1 atm). Two rearrangements are used constantly:

\[ T(P) = \frac{\Delta H}{\Delta S - R\ln(P/P_0)}, \qquad Q = \frac{\Delta H}{M_{H_2}},\ \ M_{H_2}=2.01588\ \text{g/mol}. \]
How to read it
Plot \(\ln P_{eq}\) against \(1/T\): a straight line, slope \(-\Delta H/R\), intercept \(\Delta S/R\). That is why measuring PCT curves at several temperatures gives ΔH and ΔS: take each curve's plateau pressure, plot, fit a line.

2.3Worked example: LaNi₅ Report §5.1

LaNi₅, ΔH = 32.15 kJ/mol, ΔS = 112.8 J/(mol·K)

Pressure at 25 °C (298.15 K):

\[ \ln\frac{P}{\text{atm}} = -\frac{32151}{8.314\times 298.15} + \frac{112.8}{8.314} = -12.970 + 13.567 = 0.597 \]

so \(P = e^{0.597} = 1.817\) atm \(= 1.84\) bar. LaNi₅ gives ≈ 1.8 bar at room temperature.

Temperature for 1 bar: \(\ln(1/1.01325) = -0.0132\), so

\[ T = \frac{32151}{112.8 + 8.314\times 0.0132} = \frac{32151}{112.91} = 284.8\ \text{K} = 11.6\ ^\circ\text{C}. \]

Heat per kg H₂: \(Q = 32.15/2.01588 = 15.95\) MJ/kg, which is \(15.95/119.96 = 13.3\%\) of the LHV. To release 1 kg of H₂ you must supply ≈ 16 MJ of heat, and about the same is released when refilling.

2.4Three rules of thumb

  1. \(T(1\ \text{bar}) \approx \Delta H/\Delta S\). LaNi₅: 32150/112.8 = 285 K ✓. Since ΔS is roughly constant, ΔH sets the operating temperature.
  2. 5 kJ/mol in ΔH ≈ a factor of 7.5 in pressure at 25 °C: \(e^{5000/(8.314\times298)} = e^{2.02} \approx 7.5\). This is why a model error of 5 kJ/mol is fine for sorting alloys into classes but too coarse to design a plateau pressure.
  3. Heat demand ∝ ΔH. 1 kJ/mol ≈ 0.496 MJ/kg H₂. Near-ambient intermetallics need ≈ 14–16 MJ/kg (11–13% of the LHV); MgH₂ needs 38.8 MJ/kg (32.4%).

2.6Hysteresis, slope and the NaAlH₄ check Report §5.4–5.5

Hysteresis: 82 materials state absorption ΔH, ΔS and 41 more state a hysteresis constant; in every case Pabs ≥ Pdes (no inconsistent record). Median ratio at 25 °C: 1.73.

NaAlH₄ (two plateaus): the Purdue file gives two steps, 37 and 47 kJ/mol. Which plateau is which? The digitised isotherm at 433 K settles it: the low-hydrogen plateau (6.1 bar) matches the 47 kJ/mol step Na₃AlH₆ → NaH (predicted 8.27 bar, ratio 0.74); the high-hydrogen plateau (77.8 bar) matches the 37 kJ/mol step NaAlH₄ → Na₃AlH₆ (predicted 82.2 bar, ratio 0.95). The opposite assignment would be off by ≈ 10×. Consequence: only the first step works near 30 °C; using the full 3.9 wt% needs ≈ 100 °C.

PCI consistency: refitting Van't Hoff lines to the digitised isotherms reproduces the tabulated ΔH within a median of 1.76 kJ/mol (53% within 2 kJ/mol). This is a consistency check, not independent validation, because for 32 of the fits the toolbox authors derived the tabulated value from those same curves.

2.7Estimates for new alloys: ML ΔH + assumed ΔS Report §5.6

For an alloy with no measurement, ΔH comes from the model (with its 80% interval) and ΔS is assumed to be the median of its family (e.g. AB₅: 111.2). The two uncertainties combine into a wide temperature band: about 105 K wide, ≈ 65 K from ΔH and ≈ 35 K from ΔS. Example: LaNi₄.₂Al₀.₈, predicted ΔH 38.7 kJ/mol (34.3–41.6), gives T(1 bar) ≈ 74 °C with a band of 15–124 °C. So an ML estimate can say "more stable than LaNi₅"; it cannot fix an operating window.

Defend
Q2.1Derive the Van't Hoff equation.

\(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S + RT\ln(P/P_0)\); at equilibrium \(\Delta G=0\), so \(\ln(P_{eq}/P_0) = -\Delta H/RT + \Delta S/R\). (Write it on the board; it takes 30 seconds.)

Q2.2What assumptions does it make?

(1) ΔH and ΔS do not change with temperature over the range used; (2) a flat plateau (single equilibrium pressure); (3) ideal-gas behaviour of H₂ at the pressures used. For strongly sloped plateaus, (2) fails, and a window computed from the mid-plateau is optimistic at low state of charge.

Q2.3Why is ΔS roughly constant across hydrides?

It is dominated by the loss of the gas-phase entropy of H₂ (≈ 130.7 J/mol·K standard). The solid-state contributions differ between alloys but are smaller. In the Purdue data the interquartile range is 102.8–118.9 J/mol·K.

Q2.4Why do you use P₀ = 1 atm and not 1 bar?

Because both datasets define ΔS with respect to 1 atm. The toolbox's MATLAB function uses 101 325 Pa, and HydPARK's ln P(25 °C) column is consistent with 1 atm. Using 1 bar would shift every pressure by 1.3%. Results are then converted to bar.

Q2.5Your NaAlH₄ table has two rows. Why?

NaAlH₄ decomposes in two steps with different ΔH (37 and 47 kJ/mol). I checked the assignment against the digitised 433 K isotherm: ratios of 0.74 and 0.95 between curve and prediction; the reverse assignment would be ≈ 10× off.

Q2.6Why is heat demand important?

Desorbing 1 kg H₂ from LaNi₅ needs ≈ 16 MJ, and refuelling releases about the same, which must be removed quickly. Powder beds conduct heat poorly, so heat transfer often limits the flow rate. For MgH₂ the heat is ≈ 32% of the hydrogen's LHV, a large efficiency penalty.

Q2.7How accurate is the T(1 bar) you report for a new alloy?

Not very: the band is ≈ 105 K wide, because the ΔH interval and the assumed ΔS both matter. That is exactly why new alloys are presented as candidates that need a PCT measurement.

Trap Don't present an estimated T(1 bar) as a property of the alloy.
