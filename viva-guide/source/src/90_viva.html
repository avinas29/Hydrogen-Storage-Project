Part 9Viva question bank

More questions, mixed across the project, in the order an examiner tends to escalate: basic → method → critical → traps. Answer in two or three sentences, start with the direct answer, then give one number as evidence. If you don't know, say what you would check.

Defend Level 1: basic understanding
B1What is the objective of your project?

To go from an alloy's chemical formula to a defensible statement about whether it suits a given hydrogen-storage application: a clean dataset, a ΔH model with known reliability, Van't Hoff operating windows, and a requirement-driven screening tool.

B2Why is ΔH the target, not capacity?

ΔH decides the operating temperature and heat demand, which decide whether an alloy is usable at all. Capacity data are fewer and inconsistently defined; a capacity model was tested and barely beat a physics baseline (0.247 vs 0.286 wt%), so it is not used.

B3What is a metal hydride, in one line?

A metal or alloy that absorbs hydrogen atoms into gaps in its crystal lattice, reversibly, releasing heat on charging and needing heat on discharging.

B4What data did you use?

Two public literature compilations: the Purdue Metal Hydride Toolbox (337 materials) and Sandia's ML-HydPARK v0.0.5 (772 rows). After audit and merging: 725 compositions with measured ΔH.

B5What software and libraries?

Python: pandas for data, pymatgen for composition parsing and elemental data, scikit-learn for the models and cross-validation, matplotlib for figures, Streamlit for the app, pytest for the 77 tests.

B6What is the DOE target and how do you compare?

The DOE 2025 onboard target is 5.5 wt% for the whole system (1.8 kWh/kg). Interstitial hydrides reach ≈ 1–2 wt% as a material; none comes close. Hence hydrides for stationary or heavy-duty uses.

B7What is the unit of ΔH and why "per mol H₂"?

kJ per mol of H₂ released. Normalising per H₂ lets alloys with different formulas and capacities be compared directly, and it is the quantity that enters Van't Hoff.

B8What does "applicability domain" mean?

The region of composition space where the model's predictions can be trusted, here alloys within L1 distance 0.15 of a training alloy, where the typical error is below about 5 kJ/mol.

Defend Level 2: methodology
M1Walk me through your validation.

Group near-identical compositions (same elements, L1 ≤ 0.02 → 659 groups). Outer 5-fold grouped CV repeated 3 times measures error; inside each training part a 4-fold grouped grid search chooses hyperparameters. Compare against mean and family-median baselines. Then a separate grouped 20% hold-out, leave-one-family-out tests, and data-subset refits.

M2Why compare with baselines at all?

An error number means nothing alone. A chemist's family rule already gets 8.88 kJ/mol; the model's value is the 41% improvement over that, to 5.26.

M3What hyperparameters did you tune, and why so few?

RF: max_features {0.33, 0.66}, min_samples_leaf {1, 3}; GB: learning rate and depth; ridge: α. With 725 rows a big search mostly fits noise in the inner folds. Selected RF: 300 trees, 0.33, 1.

M4How did you get the 80% interval?

From out-of-fold residuals binned by distance to the nearest training alloy: interval = prediction minus the 90% and 10% residual quantiles of that bin. For LaNi₄.₂Al₀.₈: 38.7, interval 34.3–41.6.

M5Why L1 distance and not Euclidean?

L1 on atomic fractions has a direct meaning: the total fraction of atoms you would have to change to turn one composition into the other (counted on both sides). It is simple, interpretable, and calibrated against the actual errors, which is what matters.

M6How did you merge duplicates from the two databases?

One row per composition key: Purdue single-plateau value if present, else the HydPARK median. Pseudo-elements, multi-plateau entries and suspected errors excluded but kept and flagged.

M7How did you check your thermodynamic code?

Against the toolbox's own MATLAB function (same equation), against HydPARK's ln P(25 °C) column, by hand (the LaNi₅ example), and against the digitised isotherms (median ΔH difference 1.76 kJ/mol; NaAlH₄ plateau assignment confirmed).

M8Why min–max normalisation of criteria?

It puts criteria with different units (wt%, MJ/kg, ratios) on a common 0–1 scale among the feasible materials, so weights mean "relative importance". Its weakness is sensitivity to extreme values, one reason the sensitivity analysis was done.

M9What is Kendall τ?

A rank-correlation measure: the fraction of pairs ordered the same way minus the fraction ordered differently. 1 = identical ranking. I used it to compare rankings after changing one weight.

Defend Level 3: critical questions
C1Your model fails on unseen families. So what is it good for?

Exploring substitutions within known families, which is how most hydride alloys are actually developed (Al/Sn in LaNi₅, Mn in TiFe, Ti/Zr and Cr/Mn ratios in Laves phases). There it reaches 3–4 kJ/mol, and it tells you when you have left that region.

C2Isn't the dataset biased towards AB₂ and AB₅?

Yes: AB₂ is 401 of 725 compositions. That is why errors are reported per family, why leave-family-out was run, and why predictions for sparse families are flagged. The bias reflects the literature.

C3The error is 2.4× the measurement scatter. Could a better model close the gap?

Partly. On the high-confidence subset the MAE is already 3.51, so data quality explains part of the gap. The rest is likely missing information (crystal structure, processing), which composition descriptors cannot supply. Gradient boosting gave the same MAE, so the model type is not the bottleneck.

C4Are your rankings just a product of your weights?

The order within the top group is, which I state. The membership is not: across 4000 random weight sets the same families dominate, and the top mobile alloy stays in the top 10 in 72% of completely random weightings.

C5You treat kinetics at family level. Isn't that crude?

Yes, deliberately. The individual kinetic fits span seven orders of magnitude within one rate-law group and depend on each lab's reactor, particle size and activation, so ranking single alloys by them would be misleading. Family activation energies (AB₅ ≈ 31, AB₂ ≈ 24, MgH₂ ≈ 132 kJ/mol) support only a qualitative class.

C6Why is the stationary leader TiFe-based and not LaNi₅?

TiFe₀.₉Al₀.₁ has a low, convenient plateau (1.41 bar at 25 °C) and uses cheap elements; LaNi₅ has lower capacity (1.49 wt%) and rare-earth cost. With stationary weights (cost 0.25, pressure safety 0.2), TiFe types win.

C7What did you actually contribute, if the data are public?

The audit (finding the sign, unit, leakage and overlap problems), the composition-level dataset, the honestly validated model with a calibrated domain and intervals, the Van't Hoff and PCT consistency analysis, the screening method with sensitivity analysis, and the tested software. Be ready to say which parts you did yourself and which tools you used.

C8What is novel here?

Not the random forest itself. The contribution is the end-to-end, audited workflow: leakage and overlap handled explicitly, distance-calibrated uncertainty attached to every prediction, and a transparent link from ΔH to application decisions with pass/fail/unknown logic and evidence classes.

Defend Level 4: traps (don't overclaim)
T1"So you discovered new hydrogen storage materials?"

No. The generated compositions are candidates for measurement. They lie close to known alloys by construction, ΔS is assumed, and capacity is not predicted.

T2"Your model predicts that alloy X works at 74 °C."

The model predicts ΔH only. 74 °C uses an assumed family ΔS, and the band is 15–124 °C. Only a PCT measurement fixes the operating temperature.

T3"Your interval means you are 80% sure."

It is the 10–90% range of past errors for similar distances, an empirical estimate. It is not a guarantee for one alloy, though on a check set 91% of values fell inside.

T4"Your best mobile alloy stores 2.1 wt%, so a 1 kg tank needs 48 kg."

48 kg is the alloy mass alone. A real system adds a vessel, a heat exchanger and empty space, so it would be heavier. Also, 2.1 wt% is a reported capacity of unstated definition.

T5"You validated on HydPARK, so the model generalises."

No independent validation set exists; the datasets overlap. Generalisation is estimated by grouped, nested CV within the combined data.

T6"Your cost criterion shows alloy A is cheaper."

It is a qualitative element class (common / rare earth, Co, V / precious), not a price model.

T7"You did experiments to confirm this?"

No experiments were done. All values are literature data; the experimental programme is a proposal.

T8"Your PCT check validates the tabulated ΔH."

It is a consistency check: for 32 of the fits the toolbox authors derived the tabulated value from those same digitised curves.

Defend Quick numerical checks they might ask on the board
N1What pressure does an alloy with ΔH = 30 kJ/mol, ΔS = 110 J/mol·K give at 25 °C?

\(\ln P = -30000/(8.314\times298.15) + 110/8.314 = -12.10 + 13.23 = 1.13\) → \(P\approx3.1\) atm ≈ 3.1 bar.

N2Heat to release 1 kg H₂ from a 40 kJ/mol hydride?

\(40/2.016 \approx 19.8\) MJ/kg, about 17% of the LHV.

N3Roughly at what temperature does a 40 kJ/mol, 120 J/mol·K hydride give 1 bar?

\(T \approx \Delta H/\Delta S = 40000/120 \approx 333\) K ≈ 60 °C.

N4L1 distance between TiFe and TiFe₀.₉Mn₀.₁?

Fractions (Ti, Fe, Mn): (0.5, 0.5, 0) vs (0.5, 0.45, 0.05) → 0 + 0.05 + 0.05 = 0.10. Within the domain limit.
