Part 6Screening and ranking materials

Report: §6.1–6.2, §7.3–7.5, Appendices D–E

How the project turns "here are 337 measured alloys" into "for this application, these are the best candidates, and here is why". Three ideas: hard constraints (pass/fail), a weighted score (ranking), and the Pareto front (trade-offs). Then a sensitivity test to see whether the ranking is robust.

Understand

6.1The principles

  1. Experimental data first. Rankings use the 337 Purdue materials with measured ΔH, ΔS. ML predictions are shown separately and never mixed into this ranking.
  2. Material level only. Capacities are reported material values; no tank or system design.
  3. Missing is not failing. Each constraint gives pass, fail, unknown (data missing) or not specified. A material is rejected only on a real failure.
  4. Everything visible. Constraints, criteria and weights are printed with the results and tested for sensitivity.

6.3Step 2: the weighted score

Each criterion is first scaled to 0–1 among the materials that passed (min–max normalisation):

\[ s = \frac{x - x_{\min}}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}}\ \ \text{(higher is better)},\qquad s = 1 - \frac{x - x_{\min}}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}}\ \ \text{(lower is better)} \]

then combined: \(\text{score} = \sum_k w_k s_k\) with weights summing to 1.

Kinetics class is family-level: AB₅/AB₂/AB/AB₃ = 1, complex = 0.25, Mg = 0, no data = 0.5. Individual kinetic data span 7 orders of magnitude and depend on each lab's setup, so they are not comparable alloy by alloy.

Cost class is an element indicator, not a price: common metals 1; rare earths, Co, V, minor metals 0.5; precious metals 0.

Missing hysteresis → 0.5 (neutral) and flagged.

Mobile weights favour capacity and low heat; stationary weights favour cost, low pressure and low cycling loss, because weight barely matters for a fixed store.

6.4Step 3: the Pareto front

Dominance in one sentence
Material A dominates B if A is at least as good on every objective and strictly better on one. The Pareto front is the set of materials that nobody dominates: the best possible trade-offs.

With objectives "capacity ↑" and "reaction heat ↓": in the mobile scenario, V₀.₈₅₅Ti₀.₀₉₅Fe₀.₀₅ (3.7 wt%, 21.4 MJ/kg) and TiCrMn₀.₈₅Fe₀.₃V₀.₁₅ (1.6 wt%, 10.4 MJ/kg) are both on the front: one has more capacity, the other needs half the heat. Neither is "better"; the user chooses the trade-off. The weighted score gives one ranking; the Pareto front shows the trade-off without choosing weights.

6.5Step 4: is the ranking robust? Report §6.2

  • Random weights: 2000 weight sets drawn from a Dirichlet distribution (a way to draw random positive weights that sum to 1) centred on the chosen weights, plus 2000 completely random ones. Result: the leading mobile alloy stays in the top 10 in 88% (near the chosen weights) and 72% (any weights) of draws.
  • One-at-a-time: set each weight to 0 or double it. Ranking agreement is measured with Kendall τ (1 = same order, 0 = unrelated). Largest effects: removing capacity for mobile (τ = 0.71), doubling pressure safety or removing cost for stationary (τ = 0.65).
  • Thresholds: e.g. mobile T(1 bar) limit of 60, 80 or 100 °C → 203, 232, 253 pass; the top 3 stay the same.
How to describe the result
"The membership of the top group is robust: Ti-based AB₂/AB for mobile, TiFe-type and Ti–Zr–Mn for stationary. The exact order inside the group depends on the weights, so I read the ranking as tiers, not as one winner."

Defend
Q6.1How did you choose the weights?

From the engineering priorities of each application: capacity and heat matter most for a vehicle; cost, low pressure and cycling loss for a stationary store. Because weights are a judgement, I tested them: 4000 random weight sets and one-at-a-time changes. The top group stays the same; the order within it changes.

Q6.2Why use hard constraints and a score, not just a score?

Some requirements are absolute: an alloy that cannot deliver 3 bar with 70 °C heat is useless however good its capacity. A score could hide that. So constraints remove impossible materials, and the score ranks only feasible ones.

Q6.3What is a Pareto front, and why show it as well as the ranking?

The set of materials not beaten on all objectives at once. It shows the real trade-offs (capacity vs heat) without imposing weights. The ranking needs weights; the front does not.

Q6.4Your top mobile alloy contains vanadium, which you class as costly. Contradiction?

No. Cost has a weight of 0.1 for mobile, where capacity and heat dominate. With the stationary weights (cost 0.25) V-containing alloys drop. The cost class is qualitative, not a price model.

Q6.5Is your #1 material the best hydride for vehicles?

It is the top result of a screening of literature data under stated requirements and weights. Several top entries carry data caveats, and any candidate needs its own PCT measurement, cycling and kinetics tests before use. I present the result as a short list to measure.

Trap "Best" is an overclaim; "top-ranked under these requirements" is correct.
Q6.6What happens to a material with missing hysteresis data?

It is not rejected. The hysteresis criterion gets a neutral 0.5 and the material is flagged; for the charging-pressure check, the desorption pressure is used and marked "optimistic".
