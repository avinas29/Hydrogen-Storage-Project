Understand

0.1The story in one paragraph

Hydrogen is hard to store. Some metal alloys soak it up like a sponge and release it when heated. Which alloy suits a given job (a fuel-cell vehicle, a factory buffer) depends mainly on one number, the enthalpy ΔH, because ΔH fixes the temperature at which the alloy releases hydrogen at a useful pressure. Measuring ΔH for every possible alloy is slow, so this project (1) cleaned and audited two public databases of measured hydrides, (2) trained a machine-learning model that estimates ΔH from the chemical formula alone and says how much to trust each estimate, (3) used the Van't Hoff equation to turn ΔH and ΔS into operating temperature, pressure and heat demand, and (4) built a screening tool that ranks real, measured alloys against an application's requirements. Everything is packaged in an interactive app. No lab experiments were done; a lab plan is proposed for the future.

0.2The flow of the project

Step 1 · Data: two public databases (Purdue toolbox, 337 materials; ML-HydPARK, 772 rows) → audit → one clean table of 725 compositions with measured ΔH. Report Ch 3
↓
Step 2 · Descriptors: each formula (e.g. LaNi₅) → 38 numbers computed from the elements' properties (mass, size, electronegativity …). Report §3.4
↓
Step 3 · Model: Random Forest learns descriptors → ΔH. Error MAE 5.26 kJ/mol. Every prediction gets an 80% interval and an "in/out of domain" flag. Report Ch 4
↓
Step 4 · Thermodynamics: Van't Hoff turns ΔH, ΔS into "at what temperature do I get 1 bar?" and "how much heat per kg H₂?". Report Ch 5
↓
Step 5 · Screening: requirements (temperature, pressure, mass …) → pass/fail/unknown → weighted score → Pareto front → ranked list with evidence class. Report Ch 6–7
↓
Step 6 · Platform: everything above inside an 8-page Streamlit app, "METAL HYDRIDE AI". Report §6.3

0.4What you can claim, and what you must not

You can claim
  • A composition-only model predicts ΔH of AB₅/AB₂-type alloys with MAE ≈ 5.3 kJ/mol, 41% better than a family-median rule, under honest (nested, grouped) validation.
  • The error is largest far from the training data, and you quantified this (calibration table, domain limit).
  • The public datasets have serious traps (sign conventions, back-calculated ΔS, overlap), and you found and handled them.
  • For the stated requirements, Ti-based AB₂/AB alloys rank highest for mobile use, and TiFe-type/Ti–Zr–Mn alloys for stationary use.
  • The workflow runs as a tested, interactive tool.
You must not claim
  • "The model discovers new materials." It only interpolates near known alloys; it fails on unseen families.
  • "The model predicts operating temperature." It predicts ΔH only; ΔS is assumed for new alloys.
  • "The 80% interval guarantees the true value is inside."
  • "This alloy is the best." Rankings are screening results under stated weights.
  • "2 wt% storage" as a system figure. It is material-level; a tank would be lower.
  • Any experimental result. No experiments were done.
Say this when asked "Explain your project in two minutes"

"Metal hydrides store hydrogen safely at low pressure, but which alloy suits an application depends on its enthalpy ΔH, which sets the release temperature. I combined two public hydride databases, found that they overlap heavily and use opposite sign conventions, and built a clean set of 725 compositions. I trained a random forest on 38 composition-based descriptors; under grouped, nested cross-validation it predicts ΔH with a mean error of about 5 kJ/mol, which is 41% better than a simple family rule. I measured how the error grows away from the training data, so each prediction has an interval and a reliability flag. Then I used the Van't Hoff equation to get operating temperatures and heat demand, and built a screening tool with hard constraints, weighted scores and Pareto fronts that ranks measured alloys for mobile and stationary storage. It's all in an interactive app. The model is a screening aid for alloys near known families; any candidate still needs a PCT measurement."
