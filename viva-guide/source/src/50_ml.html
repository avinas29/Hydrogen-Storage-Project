Part 5The machine-learning model

Report: §3.5 and Chapter 4, Appendix B

The part examiners probe hardest. Understand what a random forest does, why the validation is "grouped" and "nested", what the error numbers mean physically, and, most importantly, where the model stops being trustworthy.

Understand

5.1From one decision tree to a random forest background

A decision tree is a sequence of yes/no questions
"Is the mean molar volume above 9.0?" → yes → "Is the fraction of hydride formers above 0.3?" → no → … Each question splits the training alloys into two groups. At the end of the path (a "leaf"), the prediction is the average ΔH of the training alloys that ended up there. A tree can capture non-linear effects and interactions, but a single deep tree memorises noise (it "overfits").

A random forest (Breiman 2001) grows many trees and averages them. Two tricks make the trees different from each other:

  • Bootstrap: each tree trains on a random resample of the alloys (with replacement).
  • Random features: at each split, a tree may only consider a random subset of descriptors. Your model uses max_features = 0.33, i.e. about a third of the 38 at each split.

Averaging 300 different, individually overfitting trees cancels much of their noise. Your final settings: 300 trees, max_features 0.33, min_samples_leaf 1, random_state 0 (the seed, so results repeat exactly).

Why a random forest and not a neural network?
725 rows of tabular data is small. Tree ensembles are the standard strong choice for small tabular data: non-linear, no feature scaling needed, little tuning, and easy to inspect (permutation importance). A neural network would need far more data and would be harder to validate honestly. Gradient boosting (another tree ensemble) was also tested and performed the same.

5.2How good is "good"? Metrics and baselines

MAE
Mean absolute error: average of |true − predicted|. In kJ/mol. "On average the prediction is off by this much."
RMSE
Root-mean-square error. Squares the errors first, so a few large errors raise it a lot. RMSE ≫ MAE means "a few bad outliers".
R²
Share of the variation in ΔH explained. 1 = perfect, 0 = no better than always predicting the mean.

Baselines are the "dumb" predictors a model must beat to be worth anything:

  • Mean baseline: always predict the average ΔH of the training set. MAE 12.26.
  • Family-median baseline: predict the median ΔH of the alloy's family (AB₅, AB₂ …). MAE 8.88. This is what a hydride chemist's rule of thumb would give.

5.3Cross-validation: grouped and nested Report §3.5

k-fold cross-validation: split the data into 5 parts; train on 4, test on the 5th; rotate so every part is tested once; average. Repeat with 3 different shuffles. Every alloy is predicted by a model that never saw it.

Why "grouped"?
The data contain near-duplicates, e.g. LaNi₅ and LaNi₄.₉₅Sn₀.₀₅ (L1 distance 0.016). If one is in training and the other in testing, the test is too easy. So compositions with the same elements and an L1 distance ≤ 0.02 are put in the same group (725 compositions → 659 groups), and a whole group always goes to the same side of the split. Effect: ungrouped CV would have reported MAE 5.12, grouped gives 5.26, about 3% more honest.
Why "nested"?
Hyperparameters (e.g. max_features) are chosen by trying a few values and picking the best. If you pick using the same test folds you then report, the reported error is optimistically biased (Varma & Simon 2006). Nested CV has two loops: the inner loop (4-fold, inside each training part only) picks the hyperparameters; the outer loop (5-fold × 3 repeats) measures the error on data never used for any choice.

5.5What 5.26 kJ/mol means physically

  • Against the data floor: independent measurements of the same alloy differ by ≈ 2.2 kJ/mol. The model's error is ≈ 2.4× that. No model can go much below ≈ 2 with these data.
  • Against the operating point: 5 kJ/mol ≈ a factor 7.5 in plateau pressure at 25 °C. So the model can tell a 25 kJ/mol alloy (several bar at room temperature) from a 40 kJ/mol alloy (needs ≈ 100 °C), but it cannot design an exact plateau pressure. It is a screening tool.
  • Data quality: on a cleaner subset of 456 compositions (referenced, typical ΔS, metals only), MAE drops to 3.51. Part of the error is the data, not the model.

The honest conclusion
The model does not generalise to an unseen family. It interpolates within and near known families. That is why it is never used to screen Mg-based or complex hydrides, and why every prediction carries a domain flag. Saying this openly is a strength in a viva, not a weakness.

5.7Applicability domain and the 80% interval Report §4.6

Distance to the training data. For a query alloy, compute the L1 distance between its fraction vector and every training alloy's, and take the smallest, \(d_{NN}\).

Example: LaNi₄.₂Al₀.₈ vs the nearest known alloy LaNi₄.₂₅Al₀.₇₅

Fractions (La, Ni, Al): (0.167, 0.700, 0.133) vs (0.167, 0.708, 0.125). \(d_{NN} = |0.167-0.167| + |0.700-0.708| + |0.133-0.125| = 0 + 0.008 + 0.008 = 0.016.\)

5.8What the model uses: permutation importance Report §4.7

Method: take the hold-out set, shuffle one descriptor's column randomly, and see how much the error increases. A big increase means the model relies on that descriptor.

Top three: mean molar volume (+3.57 kJ/mol), fraction of hydride formers (+2.10), valence-electron spread (+1.31). The first matches the hole-size rule (Lundin 1977) and the volume descriptor Witman et al. (2020) found on HydPARK. Physically sensible. But descriptors are correlated, so importance is shared: this explains the model, not causation.

5.9The capacity model that was not used

A second model tried to predict wt% (181 Purdue records with a stated wt%). Best MAE 0.247 wt%, only slightly better than a physics baseline (0.286 wt%: take the family's typical H per metal atom and divide by the alloy's mass). The model mostly rediscovered "heavier alloys hold less wt%". With narrow ranges and inconsistent definitions, it was reported but not used. Screening uses reported capacities.

5.10The deployed model

The final RF was refitted on all 725 compositions with the same settings, then packaged unchanged into a bundle with the training compositions, the calibration table and the ΔS assumption. A manifest records the SHA-256 hash of each file (a fingerprint that changes if a single byte changes), so you can prove the app uses exactly the validated model. It was never retrained after validation.

Defend
Q5.1R² is only 0.72. Isn't that weak?

Judge it against the baselines and the data. The family rule gets R² 0.29, and independent measurements of the same alloy already scatter by ≈ 2.2 kJ/mol. R² is also pulled down by a few very stable outliers. Within AB₅/AB₂ the MAE is 3–4 kJ/mol, and on clean data 3.5. For screening that is useful; for design it is not, and I say so.

Q5.2What is overfitting and how did you avoid it?

A model that memorises training noise and fails on new data. Controlled by averaging many trees, small hyperparameter grids, and above all by measuring error only on data the model never saw (nested, grouped CV, plus a separate hold-out).

Q5.3Why 5 folds and 3 repeats?

Five folds leave 80% for training, which matters with 725 rows; three repeats with different shuffles show how stable the estimate is (± 0.14 kJ/mol). The inner loop uses 4 folds inside each training part.

Q5.4Why is the interval asymmetric for Ti₀.₅V₀.₅?

Because it uses the actual error distribution of that distance bin. In that bin some alloys were badly under-predicted (10% residual quantile −32.2 kJ/mol), so the interval stretches upward.

Q5.5Why is the domain limit 0.15 and not something else?

It comes from the calibration: up to 0.15 the MAE stays below 5 kJ/mol (2.6, 4.2, 4.6); just beyond it is 6.1, and after 0.3 it jumps to 13–17. 0.15 is where the error starts to exceed the useful screening resolution. It is a judgement based on that table.

Q5.6Is the 80% interval a confidence interval?

Not in the strict statistical sense. It is an empirical prediction interval: the 10–90% range of past out-of-fold errors for alloys at a similar distance from the training data. It assumes new alloys behave like past ones at that distance. On a check set, 91% of true values fell inside.

Q5.7What does "out-of-fold" mean?

A prediction for an alloy made by a model trained on the other folds, i.e. a model that never saw that alloy. All reported errors and the calibration use out-of-fold predictions.

Q5.8If I type LaNi₅ into your app it says 31.4, but the measured value is 32.15. Why?

LaNi₅ is in the training data, so 31.4 is an in-sample value (the app warns about this). The honest out-of-fold prediction, from a model that never saw LaNi₅, was 29.4. For training alloys, always quote the measured value.
