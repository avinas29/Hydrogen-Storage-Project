# Machine Learning Model Development

## Target and models

The target is the enthalpy of hydride decomposition, ΔH, for the 725 compositions of the union (kJ mol⁻¹ H₂, positive). The model predicts ΔH only. It does not predict ΔS, capacity, kinetics or cycle life. Three model types were compared with the two baselines of Section 3.5: ridge regression on standardised features, a random forest (RF) [@breiman2001] and gradient boosting (GB), all from scikit-learn [@pedregosa2011]. Each model was trained with the 38 composition descriptors alone and with the family one-hot columns added. The hyperparameter grids (@tab:grids) were kept small, because with 725 compositions a large search would mainly fit noise in the inner folds.

## Cross-validated performance

@tab:cv and @fig:modelcomp give the nested grouped cross-validation results. Both tree ensembles beat the baselines by a wide margin. The random forest reaches an MAE of 5.26 ± 0.14 kJ mol⁻¹, 41% lower than the family-median rule (8.88) and 57% lower than the global mean (12.26). Gradient boosting has the same MAE within one standard deviation (5.30) and a slightly better RMSE and R². Ridge regression is clearly worse (MAE 7.09), so the relation between composition and ΔH is non-linear. Adding the family label changes nothing beyond one standard deviation: the composition descriptors already encode the family.

The random forest with composition descriptors was chosen as the final model. The difference between RF and GB is within the fold-to-fold noise, and GB was not better on MAE, the primary metric. The selected RF uses 300 trees, max_features = 0.33, min_samples_leaf = 1 and random_state = 0.

With ordinary, ungrouped five-fold cross-validation the RF error would have been 5.12 kJ mol⁻¹ instead of 5.26, about 3% optimistic. The effect is small because only about 9% of the compositions have near-duplicates, but the grouped value is the one reported.

On the single grouped 20% hold-out set (145 compositions) the RF gives MAE 5.55 kJ mol⁻¹, RMSE 11.9 and R² 0.73, which agrees with the cross-validation estimate. The RMSE is about twice the MAE because a few very stable hydrides (ΔH > 60 kJ mol⁻¹) have large errors, as the parity and residual plots in @fig:parity show.

## How precise is the model?

Two references put the error in context. The first is the measurement scatter. Where the same composition is reported independently by two sources, the values differ by about 2.2 kJ mol⁻¹ on average (Section 3.2). Of the 261 union compositions that were reported more than once, 149 carry identical values copied from the same source, and the other 112 differ by a median of 1.6 kJ mol⁻¹. No model can be expected to go much below about 2 kJ mol⁻¹ with these data, so the RF error is roughly 2.4 times the measurement scatter. The second is the effect on the operating point. From @eq:vh, an error of 5 kJ mol⁻¹ in ΔH changes the plateau pressure at 25 °C by a factor of about 7.5. The model can therefore separate a 25 kJ mol⁻¹ alloy, which delivers several bar at room temperature, from a 40 kJ mol⁻¹ alloy, which needs about 100 °C. It is not precise enough to set a plateau pressure for design.

## Performance by family and within families

@tab:family breaks the out-of-fold error down by family. The model works best, in absolute and relative terms, for the two large intermetallic families. For AB₅ the MAE is 3.09 kJ mol⁻¹ against 5.15 for the family median, and for AB₂ it is 4.17 against 7.12. The model therefore captures substitution effects within a family, such as Al or Sn on the Ni site in LaNi₅ or the Ti/Zr and Cr/Mn/Fe ratios in Laves phases. Separate models trained and tested within AB₅ and AB₂ only give similar errors (3.25 and 4.23 kJ mol⁻¹; Appendix B). The errors for AB, MIC, Mg and SS are larger, and their R² values are 0.4–0.67.

## Extrapolation to unseen families

In the leave-one-family-out test, all compositions of one family were removed from training and used as the test set (@tab:lofo, @fig:lofo). The model does not generalise to an unseen family. For AB, AB₅ and SS it is *worse* than simply predicting the mean of the other families. For SS, for example, the RF MAE is 20.98 kJ mol⁻¹ against 12.33 for the mean. Where it does beat that baseline, as for Mg (14.51 against 36.01), the error is still several times larger than within-family cross-validation. Predictions are therefore meaningful only for compositions similar to the training families. For this reason Mg-based and complex hydrides are not screened with ML anywhere in this work.

## Applicability domain and prediction intervals

Because the error depends so strongly on the distance to the training data, every prediction carries a distance and an interval. The distance d~NN~ is the L1 distance between the atomic-fraction vector of the query and that of the nearest training composition. For calibration, each out-of-fold prediction was paired with its distance to the nearest training composition outside its own cross-validation group, and the residuals were binned by that distance (@tab:calib, @fig:ad). The MAE rises steadily, from 2.62 kJ mol⁻¹ for d~NN~ ≤ 0.05 to 16.9 kJ mol⁻¹ for d~NN~ > 0.5.

FIG ad | report/figures/f_applicability.png | 13 | Out-of-fold MAE against the L1 distance to the nearest training composition outside the same CV group (data from @tab:calib). Compositions beyond d~NN~ = 0.15 are flagged as outside the applicability domain.

Two rules follow from the calibration. First, the 80% prediction interval of a new composition is the predicted value plus the 10% and 90% residual quantiles of its distance bin. Second, compositions with d~NN~ > 0.15 are flagged as outside the applicability domain: beyond that point the MAE exceeds 6 kJ mol⁻¹ and grows quickly. As a check, for the 22 compositions of four substitution series (Chapter 5) that also have experimental values, the mean absolute difference between the out-of-fold prediction and the experiment is 1.91 kJ mol⁻¹, and 91% of the experimental values fall inside the nominal 80% interval. The intervals are empirical and conditional on the training data. They describe typical errors in each distance bin and are not guarantees for an individual composition.

## What the model uses

Permutation importance on the hold-out set (@fig:importance) measures how much the hold-out MAE increases when one descriptor is shuffled. The three most important descriptors are the mean molar volume (3.57 kJ mol⁻¹ increase), the fraction of hydride-forming atoms (2.10) and the spread of the valence-electron count (1.31). The first is a proxy for interstitial-site size, the hole-size effect of Lundin et al. [@lundin1977], and agrees with the volume-based descriptor reported by Witman et al. [@witman2020]. The second encodes the A:B stoichiometry, and the third is an electronic descriptor. Because many descriptors are correlated, importance is shared between them, so the ranking describes the model rather than causation.

## Sensitivity to data quality, and the capacity model

Refitting on data subsets shows that part of the error comes from the data. On a high-confidence subset of 456 compositions (referenced, typical ΔS, metals only) the RF MAE falls to 3.51 kJ mol⁻¹. Excluding very stable hydrides (ΔH > 60 kJ mol⁻¹) gives 4.08 (Appendix B).

A secondary model for gravimetric capacity was also tested on the 181 Purdue records with a stated wt%. Its best MAE, 0.247 wt%, is only slightly better than a physics baseline (0.286 wt%) that fixes H per metal atom at the family median and converts it with the alloy's mean atomic mass. With narrow capacity ranges and non-uniform definitions, this model mostly rediscovers that heavier alloys hold a smaller weight fraction. It is reported but not used; wherever capacity appears later, it is the value reported in the literature.

## Final model and deployment

The final model is the random forest refitted on all 725 compositions with the same grid, saved together with its feature list. For the software platform (Chapter 6) it was packaged unchanged, with the training compositions, the calibration table and the entropy assumption, into a single bundle. A manifest records the SHA-256 hashes of the model files and the package versions. The model was not retrained or modified after validation.
